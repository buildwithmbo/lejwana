Southern Africa's polished moment: why the region is more than rough

Lejwana · August 2026

For a century, Southern Africa's role in the diamond trade was settled: the region mined, and the world cut. Rough left Gaborone, Luanda and Windhoek for Antwerp, Tel Aviv and Surat, and the value added between rough and polished was added somewhere else.

That arrangement is ending, and it is ending by policy rather than accident. Botswana has spent two decades building beneficiation into its national strategy, requiring and incentivising cutting and polishing at home. Angola is developing polished capacity behind its state producer. Namibia markets a share of its own production directly. South Africa's transformation framework shapes who participates in its ecosystem and how. Four different countries, four different mechanisms, one direction: keep more of the stone's value where the stone came from.

The question is no longer whether Southern Africa will polish its own diamonds. It is who will be positioned when it does.

What this means for buyers

A polished stone bought from the region carries something a stone bought on a secondary market cannot: a short, documented chain from mine to sale. Fewer hands, fewer borders, fewer gaps in the record. For a trade increasingly asked to prove where its goods come from, that is not sentiment. It is a commercial advantage.

It also means new sources of supply. Polished goods are beginning to come to market from works that did not exist a decade ago, in countries that were previously rough-only. Buyers with relationships in the region will see those goods first.

Where Lejwana sits

Lejwana was founded on this shift. The house is Southern African, works inside each country's framework, and exists to connect the region's emerging polished supply to the trade that wants it. The region is more than rough. The houses built there intend to prove it.
