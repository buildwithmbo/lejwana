About HERO
# Regional polished diamonds. Clearer trade access.

We source, broker and trade natural polished diamonds across Botswana, Angola, South Africa, Namibia and Lesotho.

Based in Gaborone, we work with established jewellers, dealers, manufacturers and sourcing partners who want relevant regional stock, technically sound selection and a controlled transaction.

We work to a defined brief: sourcing through selected regional factories and suppliers, reviewing the stones put forward, and running the commercial process through to delivery.

|---|---|

|A precise brief|Sourcing against the specifications, budget, timing and end use that matter to you|
|Technical judgement|Review of make, proportions, finish, matching and overall suitability, not report data alone|
|Commercial clarity|Clear terms, available documentation and direct discussion of any limitation or compromise|

Filtered rather than listed. We do not send broad inventory feeds. We put forward stones that fit the requirement.

 
## HOW A MANDATE MOVES

### 1. Brief

You set the requirement: shape, carat weight, colour, clarity, cut, quantity, budget, destination and timing.

### 2. Source

We approach selected regional factories and suppliers for available polished stock that meets, or closely meets, the brief.

### 3. Review

We assess proposed stones against the requirement, the grading information and available supporting documentation.

### 4. Agree

Price, payment, documentation, delivery method and commercial terms are settled before goods move.

### 5. Deliver

We manage the documented, insured movement of goods through clearance, handover and settlement.

**CTA: SEND A SOURCING BRIEF**


## HOW WE OPERATE  

### Straight representation

We represent a stone as it is, not as it might be sold. If a stone does not meet the brief, if documentation is limited, or if there is a compromise to consider, we make that clear before a buyer decides.

### Direct relationships

We work through direct, repeated relationships with selected regional factories, suppliers and trade buyers. That gives buyers a better-informed route to available stock than an open marketplace can.

### Disciplined execution

Technical assessment, commercial terms, documentation, financial review and logistics are handled as one connected transaction. Selecting the stone is not the finish. The mandate closes when the stone, the records, the payment and the handover are all in order.

## THE TEAM BEHIND A MANDATE 
  
A buyer does not need four separate service providers. The team works together around the same brief: sourcing, technical selection, commercial review and controlled delivery.

- Use "we" for company and process copy; third person for biographies.

2. Brand and visual direction

Overall character

The page should feel calm, exacting, editorial and trade-literate. Avoid the usual luxury-jewellery cliché: black backgrounds, large diamond photography, glitter effects, metallic gradients and overly decorative serif typography. Lejwana should look closer to a serious specialist publication or commodity-intelligence desk than a jewellery retailer.

The visual idea is “a precise cut through regional trade information.” Use thin rules, strong grids, restrained typography, quiet earth-and-stone tones and one mineral-blue accent.

  
Image direction

Use documentary, not advertising, photography:

- Aerial or landscape imagery of Botswana, Namibia, Angola, Lesotho or South Africa.
    
- Diamond sorting, cutting, polishing, parcels, hands at work, maps, documentation and logistics.
    
- Architectural details from Gaborone, Saurimo, Windhoek, Johannesburg and Maseru.
    
- Avoid stock images of engagement rings unless a jewellery-market story specifically requires it.
    

For the hero, use one low-contrast, wide image treated with an ivory or charcoal overlay. A close crop of rough diamonds or a regional landscape is stronger than a polished solitaire.

- “Source checked” label on time-sensitive items.
    

Responsive behaviour

- Desktop: 12-column grid, maximum content width of 1,280–1,440 px.
    
- Tablet: two-column cards where practical; calendar becomes scrollable or moves to a two-line card format.
    
- Mobile: one-column layout; one featured story then stacked cards; category filters scroll horizontally; calendar uses expandable cards.
    
- Keep the trade-calendar call to action visible near the top on every screen size.
    
- Do not place essential trade information only in hover states.
    

Accessibility

- Meet WCAG AA contrast standards.
    
- Use large, readable text and generous line height.
    
- Never communicate status by colour alone; always include text and an icon where needed.
    
- Make all cards and buttons keyboard-accessible.
    
- Use meaningful alt text for documentary images.
    
- Mark external links visibly and accessibly.
    


7. Content and editorial rules

What belongs on the hub

- Confirmed sales dates, auction dates, viewing periods and tender notices.
    
- Producer results, production guidance, significant recoveries, maintenance and mine-plan changes.
    
- Price signals from named producers or disclosed auction results.
    
- Policy with a direct bearing on diamond production, trade, certification, export, ownership, taxation or beneficiation.
    
- Regional mining and jewellery events with clear commercial relevance.
    
- Explainers that help readers understand the market.
    

What does not belong

- Generic luxury or celebrity diamond content.
    
- Unsourced price claims.
    
- Old events displayed as future dates.
    
- Broad conferences with no diamond or trade relevance.
    
- Rewritten press releases without a regional implication.
    

Language style

- Clear, informed and specific.
    
- Use plain English before technical language.
    
- Define specialist terms on first use when writing for mixed-experience audiences.
    
- Do not overuse “luxury,” “prestige,” “sparkle,” “timeless” or similar jewellery-marketing language.
    
- State uncertainty honestly: “awaiting official confirmation,” “company guidance,” “reported,” “estimated,” or “not publicly disclosed.”
    

Date verification standard

Every calendar entry must include an official source link and a “last checked” date. Where the organiser changes an event date, show Updated and retain the correction in the entry history.

For pricing, label the source, period, sales channel and diamond category. Never present a single producer average as the whole region’s price.

## 01 — What is a Sight?

**Standfirst:** How De Beers' scheduled rough sales work.

A Sight is one of De Beers' scheduled rough-diamond sales. Instead of auctioning stones, De Beers sells to a defined group of customers, known as Sightholders, on a fixed calendar through the year. [CONFIRM: number of Sights per year for 2026]

Each Sightholder is allocated a box of rough in the assortments it has agreed to buy. The buyer inspects the goods, then decides whether to take them at the price offered. Prices are set by De Beers rather than discovered through bidding. [CONFIRM: current wording of De Beers' allocation and refusal rules]

Because the calendar is public, Sights are useful signposts. They tell the market when a large share of rough supply is changing hands, and what buyers do with it afterwards often shows in secondary-market premiums and discounts.

Access is by contract. If you are not a Sightholder, the stones you see have usually passed through a Sightholder or another sales channel first. [CONFIRM: how De Beers describes eligibility]

## 02 — Auction, tender or contract sale?

**Standfirst:** The main ways rough reaches buyers.

Rough diamonds reach manufacturers through three main routes.

**Contract sales** are agreed in advance between a producer and a registered customer. Volume, assortment and timing are set by the agreement, and pricing follows the producer's list. This gives the buyer a steady supply and the seller a predictable outlet. The Sight system is the best-known example.

**Auctions** are open competitive sales. Registered buyers inspect the lots, then bid, and the highest bid wins. Spot auctions, such as those run by the Okavango Diamond Company, price the goods as the market sees them on the day.

**Tenders** are sealed-bid sales. Buyers view the goods, then submit one confidential price per lot. The seller awards each lot to the best bid. Tenders are common for larger or exceptional stones, where a single price matters more than a rolling set of bids.

Each route suits different goods and different buyers. Most producers use more than one. [CONFIRM: any producer-specific examples to name]

## 03 — Rough, polished and yield

**Standfirst:** What a rough parcel really becomes.

A rough diamond is not a small polished one. Cutting removes most of the original weight, and the share that stays is called the yield.

Yield depends on the shape and quality of the rough and on what the manufacturer is aiming to produce. A well-formed crystal can give a high yield. A flat, included or irregular one gives far less. [CONFIRM: whether to give indicative yield ranges, and from which source]

This is why rough is priced by what it can make, not by weight alone. A manufacturer looks at a parcel and works backwards. They estimate the polished it will yield, the likely value of that polished, and the cost of getting there, then arrive at a price they can pay.

For a polished buyer, the point is simple. A polished stone's price already carries the weight lost in cutting, the labour and the manufacturer's margin. Understanding yield explains why polished prices move differently from rough.

## 04 — What does provenance prove?

**Standfirst:** What the paperwork shows, and what it does not.

Provenance is a record of where a diamond came from and who has held it since. At its strongest, it ties a stone to a mine or producer and to the documents it picked up on the way to market.

For rough, this usually means export and import permits and a Kimberley Process certificate, which states that a parcel has been handled under the scheme's rules. [CONFIRM: Kimberley Process wording against the current KP definitions] For polished, it can include producer or manufacturer records and a grading report.

What it proves is limited. A certificate shows that the paperwork exists and is consistent. It does not by itself prove a diamond's origin once rough from several sources has been mixed or cut. Many polished stones cannot be traced to a single mine, and a claim that they can should be supported by records, not by assertion.

For buyers, the useful question is how far back the records go, who vouches for each step, and whether the documents match the goods.

## 05 — How to read price per carat

**Standfirst:** Why one average can mislead.

Price per carat is the headline figure in rough and polished reports. It is also easy to misread.

A carat is a unit of weight. A price per carat is the total value divided by the total weight, so it is an average over everything in the sale. If a producer sells a different mix of sizes and qualities from one period to the next, the average will move even if no individual price has changed.

That is why a rising average can reflect richer goods and not stronger prices, and a falling one can reflect a larger share of smaller or lower-quality stones.

To read the figure properly, look for:

- the assortment or mix being sold
- the size ranges and quality bands
- whether the figure is for rough or polished
- the currency, and whether it is a list, auction or tender price

Comparing like with like, such as the same size and quality across two sales, tells you far more than a single average. [CONFIRM: whether to add a regional example from a named producer]

## 06 — From Botswana to the buyer

**Standfirst:** The trade, logistics and documentation journey.

A diamond's route from mine to buyer is a chain of handovers, and each one leaves a record.

It starts at the mine, where rough is recovered and sorted. In Botswana, a share of production is sold through the Okavango Diamond Company and the rest through De Beers contract sales. [CONFIRM: current sales structure and wording]

From there, goods move under export permits, with a Kimberley Process certificate, to a sorting and sales centre, then to a manufacturer. Manufacturing may happen in Botswana, or in another cutting centre such as India. [CONFIRM: which centres to name]

Once polished, stones are graded and documented, then sold to dealers, jewellers and brand owners. Each move depends on insured transit, customs clearance and matching paperwork at both ends.
