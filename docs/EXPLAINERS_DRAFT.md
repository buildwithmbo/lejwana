## 01 — What is a Sight?

**Standfirst:** How De Beers' scheduled rough sales work.

A Sight is one of De Beers' scheduled rough-diamond sales. Instead of auctioning stones, De Beers sells to a defined group of customers, known as Sightholders, on a fixed calendar through the year. [CONFIRM: number of Sights per year for 2026]

Each Sightholder is allocated a box of rough in the assortments it has agreed to buy. The buyer inspects the goods, then decides whether to take them at the price offered. Prices are set by De Beers rather than discovered through bidding. [CONFIRM: current wording of De Beers' allocation and refusal rules]

Because the calendar is public, Sights are useful signposts. They tell the market when a large share of rough supply is changing hands, and what buyers do with it afterwards often shows in secondary-market premiums and discounts.

Access is by contract. If you are not a Sightholder, the stones you see have usually passed through a Sightholder or another sales channel first. [CONFIRM: how De Beers describes eligibility]

## 02 — Auction, tender or contract sale?

**Standfirst:** The main ways rough reaches buyers.

Rough diamonds reach manufacturers through three main routes.

**Contract sales** are agreed in advance between a producer and a registered customer. Volume, assortment and timing are set by the agreement, and pricing follows the producer's list. This gives the buyer a steady supply and the seller a predictable outlet. The Sight system is the best-known example.

**Auctions** are open competitive sales. Registered buyers inspect the lots, then bid, and the highest bid wins. Spot auctions, such as those run by the Okavango Diamond Company, price the goods as the market sees them on the day.

**Tenders** are sealed-bid sales. Buyers view the goods, then submit one confidential price per lot. The seller awards each lot to the best bid. Tenders are common for larger or exceptional stones, where a single price matters more than a rolling set of bids.

Each route suits different goods and different buyers. Most producers use more than one. [CONFIRM: any producer-specific examples to name]

## 03 — Rough, polished and yield

**Standfirst:** What a rough parcel really becomes.

A rough diamond is not a small polished one. Cutting removes most of the original weight, and the share that stays is called the yield.

Yield depends on the shape and quality of the rough and on what the manufacturer is aiming to produce. A well-formed crystal can give a high yield. A flat, included or irregular one gives far less. [CONFIRM: whether to give indicative yield ranges, and from which source]

This is why rough is priced by what it can make, not by weight alone. A manufacturer looks at a parcel and works backwards. They estimate the polished it will yield, the likely value of that polished, and the cost of getting there, then arrive at a price they can pay.

For a polished buyer, the point is simple. A polished stone's price already carries the weight lost in cutting, the labour and the manufacturer's margin. Understanding yield explains why polished prices move differently from rough.

## 04 — What does provenance prove?

**Standfirst:** What the paperwork shows, and what it does not.

Provenance is a record of where a diamond came from and who has held it since. At its strongest, it ties a stone to a mine or producer and to the documents it picked up on the way to market.

For rough, this usually means export and import permits and a Kimberley Process certificate, which states that a parcel has been handled under the scheme's rules. [CONFIRM: Kimberley Process wording against the current KP definitions] For polished, it can include producer or manufacturer records and a grading report.

What it proves is limited. A certificate shows that the paperwork exists and is consistent. It does not by itself prove a diamond's origin once rough from several sources has been mixed or cut. Many polished stones cannot be traced to a single mine, and a claim that they can should be supported by records, not by assertion.

For buyers, the useful question is how far back the records go, who vouches for each step, and whether the documents match the goods.

## 05 — How to read price per carat

**Standfirst:** Why one average can mislead.

Price per carat is the headline figure in rough and polished reports. It is also easy to misread.

A carat is a unit of weight. A price per carat is the total value divided by the total weight, so it is an average over everything in the sale. If a producer sells a different mix of sizes and qualities from one period to the next, the average will move even if no individual price has changed.

That is why a rising average can reflect richer goods and not stronger prices, and a falling one can reflect a larger share of smaller or lower-quality stones.

To read the figure properly, look for:

- the assortment or mix being sold
- the size ranges and quality bands
- whether the figure is for rough or polished
- the currency, and whether it is a list, auction or tender price

Comparing like with like, such as the same size and quality across two sales, tells you far more than a single average. [CONFIRM: whether to add a regional example from a named producer]

## 06 — From Botswana to the buyer

**Standfirst:** The trade, logistics and documentation journey.

A diamond's route from mine to buyer is a chain of handovers, and each one leaves a record.

It starts at the mine, where rough is recovered and sorted. In Botswana, a share of production is sold through the Okavango Diamond Company and the rest through De Beers contract sales. [CONFIRM: current sales structure and wording]

From there, goods move under export permits, with a Kimberley Process certificate, to a sorting and sales centre, then to a manufacturer. Manufacturing may happen in Botswana, or in another cutting centre such as India. [CONFIRM: which centres to name]

Once polished, stones are graded and documented, then sold to dealers, jewellers and brand owners. Each move depends on insured transit, customs clearance and matching paperwork at both ends.
